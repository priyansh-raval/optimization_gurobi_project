\section*{Question 1: Minimize Mean Travel Time}

Select $p$ of $N$ stores to minimize the average travel time of $M$ customers.
Let $[M]=\{1,\ldots,M\}$ and $[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}$, and let $t_{ij}$ be the travel time from customer $i$ to store $j$.

\subsection*{Decision Variables}
\[
y_j = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if store $j$ is selected},\\
0 & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
x_{ij} = \begin{cases}
1 & \text{if customer $i$ is assigned to store $j$},\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Both variables are binary, for all $i\in[M]$ and $j\in[N]$.

\subsection*{Objective Function}
\begin{equation}
\min_{y,x}\quad \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}t_{ij}x_{ij}.
\label{eq:mean-obj}
\end{equation}
This minimizes the mean travel time to customers' assigned stores. Each customer is
assigned to a nearest selected store in an optimal solution.

\subsection*{Constraints}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.49\textwidth}p{0.47\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Constraint} & \textbf{What it enforces} \\
\midrule
$\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_j=p$ & Select exactly $p$ stores. \\[6pt]
$\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{ij}=1\quad \forall i\in[M]$ & Assign each customer to exactly one store. \\[6pt]
$x_{ij}\le y_j\quad \forall i\in[M],\ j\in[N]$ & Allow assignments only to selected stores. \\[6pt]
$y_j\in\{0,1\}\quad \forall j\in[N]$ & Make store selection binary. \\[6pt]
$x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\quad \forall i\in[M],\ j\in[N]$ & Make customer assignments binary. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section*{Question 2: Minimize Worst-Case Travel Time}

Select $p$ stores to minimize the maximum customer travel time, using the same $t_{ij}$.

\subsection*{Decision Variables}
Use the binary variables $y_j$ and $x_{ij}$ from Question 1, but add a continuous variable
$T\ge0$ that bounds every customer's travel time.

\subsection*{Objective Function}
\begin{equation}
\min_{y,x,T}\quad T.
\label{eq:max-obj}
\end{equation}
This minimizes the worst-case customer travel time; at optimality, $T$ equals the largest assigned travel time.

\subsection*{Constraints}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.49\textwidth}p{0.47\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Constraint} & \textbf{What it enforces} \\
\midrule
$\sum_{j=1}^{N}t_{ij}x_{ij}\le T\quad \forall i\in[M]$ & Bound every customer's travel time by $T$. \\[6pt]
$\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_j=p$ & Select exactly $p$ stores. \\[6pt]
$\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{ij}=1\quad \forall i\in[M]$ & Assign each customer to exactly one store. \\[6pt]
$x_{ij}\le y_j\quad \forall i\in[M],\ j\in[N]$ & Allow assignments only to selected stores. \\[6pt]
$y_j\in\{0,1\}\quad \forall j\in[N]$ & Make store selection binary. \\[6pt]
$x_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\quad \forall i\in[M],\ j\in[N]$ & Make customer assignments binary. \\
$T\ge0$ & Keep the travel-time bound nonnegative. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section*{Question 4: Average and worst-case travel time versus $p$}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{part1_mean_max_time.png}
    \caption{Mean (solid) and maximum (dashed) customer travel time versus $p$ for the mean model (blue) and worst-case model (green). For both models, travel times are measured to each customer's closest selected store.}
    \label{fig:mean-max}
\end{figure}

The average and worst-case travel times generally decrease as we include more locations in the pilot, which makes sense. From $p=1$ to $p=10$, the mean-travel-time model's average falls from 18.12 to 7.34 minutes, while its maximum falls from 56.00 to 29.40 minutes. The worst-case model's average falls from 18.97 to 8.82 minutes, and its maximum falls from 45.80 to 25.40 minutes. This effect can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:mean-max}.

The largest improvements occur as $p$ increases from 1 to 5, with more modest improvements afterward. In particular, the worst-case model's maximum stays at 25.40 minutes for $p\ge5$. This reflects saturation: even selecting all 36 stores cannot improve the maximum travel time on this dataset.

Figure~\ref{fig:mean-max} also shows that the worst-case model has a smaller gap between its mean and maximum travel times. Its maximum is lower, but its mean is higher than the mean model's. This illustrates the tradeoff between reducing average travel time and protecting the customers with the longest trips.

Figure~\ref{fig:maps} shows the two $p = 5$ solutions and Table~\ref{tab:p5} summarizes them. 
The two models select three of the same stores: Store 1 (lower Manhattan), Store 7 (upper Manhattan),
and Store 31 (Staten Island). But the more interesting analysis is how the models differ in their 
selection of the other two stores. The `mean-dist' model places the other two stores at the centers of the two other large customer 
clusters: store 23 in central Brooklyn and store 35 in Queens (Figure~\ref{fig:map-mean-store}). 
Customers are split fairly evenly across stores 1, 7, 23 and 35, and approximately 60\% of customers are within 
10~minutes of a pilot store (Figure~\ref{fig:map-mean-time}).


However, our `max-dist' moves them south-east, to store 16 in eastern Brooklyn and store 24 in southern 
Brooklyn (Figure~\ref{fig:map-max-store}). It does this to reach the most remote customers on the 
south-eastern edge of the region. Store 16 is the closest pilot store for the 25.4-minute 
bottleneck customer. As a result, this model leaves Queens without a pilot store. The 637 customers 
that `mean-dist' serves from store 35 are split between stores 16, 1, 7, and 24, and their average travel time 
rises from 11.3 to 16.5~minutes (the green and yellow area in Figure~\ref{fig:map-max-time}). Store 1 
becomes the closest pilot store for 1,270 customers (42\% of all customers), and the share of customers 
with a trip longer than 15~minutes rises from 6.3\% to 22.6\%. The `mean-dist' model's worst case comes 
from the same remote edge: its 31.7-minute customer is in the Rockaways (the yellow strip in 
Figure~\ref{fig:map-mean-time}).


We recommend the mean-dist solution for this pilot because it offers shorter trips for most customers, which may encourage participation and help reduce delivery costs. Compared with the max-dist solution, it increases the share of customers within 15~minutes of a pilot store from 77.4\% to 93.7\% and reduces average travel time by 18\%, from 11.28 to 9.29~minutes. The tradeoff is a longer maximum trip: 31.7 rather than 25.4~minutes. However, only 1.6\% of customers face trips longer than 20~minutes. For this pilot, we believe improving convenience for more customers outweighs reducing the longest trip by 6.3~minutes.

\section*{Question 6: Travel distance instead of travel time}

Travel distances can easily be incorporated by replacing $t_{ij}$ with distances
$d_{ij}$ in the mean model's objective and the worst-case model's travel constraints.
The decision variables and remaining constraints stay unchanged, and $T$ represents
maximum travel distance rather than time.

However, using distance is not necessarily an improvement. It avoids relying on
traffic-free travel-time estimates, but does not capture congestion or how long
customers spend traveling. Since customer convenience depends on travel time,
traffic-aware travel-time estimates would address the colleague's concern more directly.
\end{minipage}
